# Average Order Value

> [!summary]
> **Average Order Value (AOV)** is the average amount a customer spends in a single order. In affiliate marketing it is the hidden multiplier on percentage-based commissions: under [[Cost per Sale]], your earnings are `commission % × order value`, so a higher AOV means more money per sale at the *same* commission rate.

## The formula

```
AOV = total revenue ÷ number of orders
```

Example: a store does $50,000 across 400 orders → `50000 ÷ 400 = $125` AOV.

## Why it matters to an affiliate

Under percentage [[Cost per Sale]], the commission rate alone tells you nothing about your real earnings. AOV is the other half:

```
earnings per sale = commission % × AOV
```

> [!tip] A lower rate on high AOV beats a higher rate on low AOV
> - 5% commission on a $2,000 order = **$100**
> - 10% commission on a $40 order = **$4**
>
> The 5% offer pays 25× more per sale despite the lower headline rate. Always weigh the percentage *against* the niche's typical AOV before picking a program.

## AOV's effect on the whole funnel

AOV feeds directly into [[Earnings per Click]], the metric for comparing offers:

```
EPC = conversion rate × commission % × AOV
```

So a niche with **high AOV** can be worth promoting even with mediocre traffic or a modest commission rate — each conversion simply pays more. This is why furniture, electronics, mattresses, and B2B software are attractive affiliate niches: a single sale is large.

## What drives AOV up

Merchants (and you, indirectly) raise AOV through:

- **Upsells** — a pricier version of the chosen product.
- **Cross-sells** — complementary add-ons ("frequently bought together").
- **Bundles** — package deals that lift the basket.
- **Free-shipping thresholds** — "$10 more for free shipping" nudges bigger carts.
- **Premium / tiered pricing** — higher-end SKUs in the range.

As an affiliate you benefit automatically from a merchant with high AOV — you earn on the *whole* order the customer places, not just the one item you linked (under most last-click [[Attribution Model|attribution]] and order-level commission terms).

## Worked example — AOV deciding between two programs

You're choosing which standing-desk brand to promote:

| Brand | Commission | Typical AOV | Earnings/sale |
|-------|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| Brand A | 12% | $250 | **$30** |
| Brand B | 6% | $900 | **$54** |

Brand B's lower rate wins because its AOV is 3.6× higher. Without AOV in the comparison you'd have wrongly picked Brand A.

## Common gotchas

> [!warning] Order-level vs item-level commission
> Some programs pay commission on the **entire order value** (you benefit from the customer's whole basket); others pay only on the **specific item** you referred. Read the terms — it changes whether high AOV actually helps you.

- **Category-capped rates** — a program may pay high % on cheap categories and low % on expensive ones, neutralising the AOV advantage (common on Amazon).
- **Refunds shrink realised AOV** — see [[Reversal]]; a returned high-value order claws back a large commission.
- **AOV ≠ profit** — high AOV often means longer consideration and lower conversion rates; balance it against [[Earnings per Click]], not in isolation.

## Related terms

- [[Cost per Sale]] — the model where AOV multiplies your percentage commission.
- [[Earnings per Click]] — the comparison metric AOV feeds into.
- [[Cost per Action]] — the umbrella family; AOV is irrelevant to flat-fee CPA/[[Cost per Lead]], only to %-of-sale payouts.
- [[Attribution Model]] — determines whether you earn on the whole order.
- [[Reversal]] — refunds on high-AOV orders cost the most.
